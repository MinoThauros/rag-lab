# Private RAG Wins on Plumbing, Not Models

As of late 2026, retrieval-augmented generation is a mature engineering discipline with a settled default architecture. Parse documents with a layout-aware parser, split them along their natural structure, prepend document context to each chunk, retrieve with BM25 plus dense vectors fused together, rerank with a cross-encoder, and generate with citations. Almost every serious source agrees on that baseline, and on the fact that the parts that decide success sit around it rather than inside it: parsing messy documents, permissions, an evaluation set built with subject-matter experts, and an owner for the system after handoff. The model choice, the vector database and clever prompting matter much less than consultants and clients tend to assume. The market has also moved: in 2025, 76% of enterprises bought AI solutions rather than building them ([Menlo Ventures](https://menlovc.com/perspective/2025-the-state-of-generative-ai-in-the-enterprise/)), and horizontal copilots now absorb generic "chat with our documents." A private-RAG consultant in Quebec therefore has a real but specific niche. The work is regulated or residency-sensitive mid-size organizations, domain corpora full of tables and exact identifiers, workflow-embedded use cases with a measurable outcome, and readiness work (permissions cleanup, evaluating a bought tool) that clients need whether they build or buy. Your existing stack, TypeScript, Postgres and vLLM serving Qwen models, maps almost one-to-one onto a credible reference architecture. The gaps you need to close are Python-side document parsing, permission sync and evaluation practice, not new infrastructure.

## Ingestion is where answer quality is won or lost

Ingestion means getting text and structure out of source files, cleaning it, cutting it into retrievable pieces and labelling those pieces. Production teams consistently name it as the bottleneck. Uber's internal policy copilot gave incomplete or wrong answers until the team discovered that **no off-the-shelf PDF loader worked**, moved the documents to Google Docs, wrote a custom loader that preserved tables and tables of contents, and added LLM-generated summaries, FAQs and keywords per chunk. Together with BM25 and query rewriting, that produced **27% more acceptable answers and 60% fewer incorrect ones**, while tuning prompts and retrieval parameters "plateaued quickly" ([Uber](https://www.uber.com/blog/enhanced-agentic-rag/)). That pattern is the most useful single lesson for a new consultant: the improvement came from the documents, not the model.

### Parsing: digital text is solved, scans and tables are not

A born-digital PDF (one exported from Word, not scanned) is essentially a solved problem. On AllenAI's olmOCR-Bench, which grades parsers with pass/fail unit tests, the "base" simple-text category scores about 99 for nearly every system, while **"old scans" sits between 29 and 50 for every system tested** ([olmOCR](https://github.com/allenai/olmocr)). That gap is the clearest numeric picture of what remains open. Tables with merged cells, multi-column reading order, formulas and charts lie between those extremes.

The big shift since 2025 is that small, specialized vision-language OCR models have overtaken classic pipeline parsers. On OmniDocBench, the de facto independent leaderboard for layout-rich documents, the top scorers are models like **PaddleOCR-VL-1.6 (96.34), MinerU2.5-Pro (95.75) and GLM-OCR (95.22)**. They beat general frontier models such as Gemini 3 Pro (92.91) and GPT-5.2 (86.59), and classic tools such as Marker (78.44) ([OmniDocBench](https://github.com/opendatalab/OmniDocBench)). The leaders are only about 0.9B to 1.2B parameters, so they are cheap to self-host ([Roboflow](https://roboflow.com/blog/best-open-source-ocr-models)). olmOCR itself is Apache-2.0, needs a 12 GB+ NVIDIA GPU and costs **under $200 per million pages** to self-host ([olmOCR](https://github.com/allenai/olmocr)). That is within reach of a 16 GB card like your 5070 Ti.

Two caveats keep this from being a simple "pick the winner" decision. The two benchmarks disagree: MinerU tops OmniDocBench, which is heavy on Chinese and English academic pages, but sits below Marker on olmOCR-Bench ([olmOCR](https://github.com/allenai/olmocr)). And the leaderboard churns roughly every quarter. The practical consequence is architectural. Put the parser behind an interface so it can be swapped, and choose it by running a few dozen of the client's own ugliest documents through two or three candidates.

**Docling** (MIT, IBM Research, now under LF AI & Data) is the safest default for breadth. It handles PDF, DOCX, PPTX, XLSX, HTML, email and images, runs air-gapped, and ships **docling-serve**, an HTTP API server, which matters for a TypeScript shop ([Docling](https://github.com/docling-project/docling)). Its accuracy on hard PDFs is less clear. Marker's maker reports Marker beating Docling 76.0 to 50.3 on olmOCR-Bench, but that is a vendor-run comparison using Docling's default pipeline ([via themenonlab](https://themenonlab.blog/blog/best-open-source-pdf-to-markdown-tools-2026)). Managed alternatives include **Mistral OCR 3 at $2 per 1,000 pages** ($1 batch), with a self-hosting option for privacy-sensitive customers ([Mistral](https://mistral.ai/news/mistral-ocr-3)), along with Azure Document Intelligence, Textract, Google Document AI, LlamaParse and Reducto. On Reducto's own table benchmark it scored 90.2 against Azure's 82.7 and Textract's 80.9, but Reducto ran the benchmark ([Reducto](https://reducto.ai/blog/evaluating-azure-document-intelligence-for-pdf-parsing)).

Ingestion remains Python territory: Docling, MinerU, Marker, PaddleOCR-VL and olmOCR are all Python and GPU-centric. LlamaIndex's March 2026 **LiteParse** gives TypeScript a native local parser ([MarkTechPost](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/03/19/llamaindex-releases-liteparse-a-cli-and-typescript-native-library-for-spatial-pdf-parsing-in-ai-agent-workflows/)), but pure-JS parsing is only competitive on clean digital PDFs. The pattern that fits you is a containerized Python parsing service behind a queue, writing normalized output to object storage, with everything downstream in TypeScript. Keep the raw files and the parsed intermediates. Re-parsing is the expensive GPU step, and keeping its output lets you re-chunk or re-embed cheaply later.

### Cleaning is mostly absorbed by good parsers

Modern layout-aware parsers classify headers, footers and page numbers as layout elements and drop them. olmOCR-Bench scores this explicitly, and the spread is wide: **PaddleOCR-VL scores 97.0, MinerU 96.6 and Marker 86.6, while Nanonets-OCR2-3B scores only 32.1** ([olmOCR](https://github.com/allenai/olmocr)). Picking a parser that scores well here removes most of the custom regex cleanup. Beyond that, cleaning is standard hygiene: encoding repair, Unicode normalization, exact-hash and near-duplicate removal, and PII redaction with a tool like Microsoft Presidio. These are practitioner defaults rather than findings from this research, and no 2025–2026 study quantified their effect on retrieval quality. This is a genuine gap in the evidence, not a sign the steps don't matter.

### Chunking: respect structure, stop agonizing over size

Chunking is how you cut documents into pieces small enough to retrieve precisely but large enough to carry meaning. The evidence converges more than the volume of blog posts suggests. The largest recent sweep, from the University of Canberra, tested 36 strategies across 6 domains and 5 embedding models (1,080 configurations). **Grouping two paragraphs with one-paragraph overlap scored best (nDCG@5 about 0.459), while fixed 100-character chunks scored about 0.244**, and no strategy won every domain ([arXiv 2603.06976](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.06976v1)). Vectara concluded that embedding-based "semantic chunking" does not justify its compute cost ([arXiv 2410.13070](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.13070v1)). NVIDIA found page-level chunks best for paginated reports, but by a small margin ([NVIDIA](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/finding-the-best-chunking-strategy-for-accurate-ai-responses/)).

In plain terms, the big loss comes from doing something dumb, like tiny character-count chunks that slice sentences in half. Among sensible strategies the differences are a few points. The 2026 default is structure-aware splitting at roughly 200–512 tokens. Each chunk carries its heading path ("Policy Manual > 4. Leave > 4.2 Parental leave"), tables stay whole, and the split size is tuned on the client's own evaluation set. Factoid questions favor smaller chunks and synthesis questions favor larger ones.

### Enrichment is the best-evidenced ingestion upgrade

The technique with the strongest evidence is giving each chunk context about the document it came from before indexing it. Anthropic's **Contextual Retrieval** has an LLM write 50–100 tokens explaining where a chunk sits in its document ("This clause is from Acme's 2024 supplier agreement, section on termination…") and prepends that text before both embedding and BM25 indexing. It cut top-20 retrieval failures from 5.7% to 3.7% on its own, to 2.9% with BM25 added, and to **1.9% with reranking, a 67% reduction**, at about $1 per million document tokens with prompt caching ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval)). Anthropic measured this on its own datasets and no independent replication surfaced, but the direction is widely corroborated. Cheaper variants exist. "Late chunking" embeds the whole document and pools token vectors per chunk, for roughly a 3% relative gain with no LLM calls ([arXiv 2409.04701](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.04701v3)). Deterministic headers (title plus section path) cost nothing at all. For a private deployment, your local Qwen model can do the contextualization during ingestion. The hidden cost is re-running it over the whole corpus whenever you change the prompt.

Metadata is not optional either. Every chunk needs a document ID, source URI, section path, page, dates, document type, language, ACL principals, content hash and parser/chunker version. Those fields are what make permission filtering, deletion and targeted re-parsing possible.

### Re-indexing is solved engineering that teams still get wrong

Keeping the index in sync with changing sources is a known pattern: a ledger of content hashes per document and per chunk, skip what is unchanged, replace what changed, delete what disappeared. LangChain's indexing API is the most documented open implementation, and it shows the classic trap. **Only "full" cleanup mode deletes chunks from removed sources, and it requires the loader to return the entire dataset**, or it will delete valid documents ([LangChain](https://python.langchain.com/docs/how_to/indexing)). In Postgres the simpler answer is a `documents` table holding hash, version and status, with chunks foreign-keyed to it and cascade-deleted. That gives you transactional consistency almost for free. Two refinements are worth adopting. Compare hashes at chunk level, so that editing one paragraph doesn't re-embed a 300-page manual. And treat any change of parser, chunker or embedding model as a full blue/green re-index.

## Hybrid search plus reranking is the default retrieval baseline

By 2026, "BM25 plus dense vectors, fused with reciprocal rank fusion, then a cross-encoder reranker" is the standard starting point, not an advanced option ([noqta.tn](https://noqta.tn/en/blog/vector-database-comparison-2026-pgvector-pinecone-qdrant-weaviate)). The intuition is worth explaining to clients. A dense vector catches paraphrase, so "cancel my subscription" finds "terminating your plan." BM25 catches exact tokens that embeddings blur, such as "article 17", "SKU-44-B", an error code or a person's name. Reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) merges the two ranked lists by summing 1/(k + rank) for each document. It needs no score calibration, which is why it became the default. A reranker then reads the query and each candidate *together*. That is far more accurate than comparing two independently computed vectors, but too slow to run over the whole corpus. So you retrieve 50–150 candidates cheaply and rerank down to 5–20. Anthropic's recommendation is to retrieve 150 and pass 20 ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval)). The winning TREC RAG 2025 retrieval system stacked exactly this: sparse plus dense retrieval, HyDE and LLM reranking ([TREC 2025 UTokyo](https://trec.nist.gov/pubs/trec34/papers/UTokyo.rag.pdf)).

### Embeddings: shortlist, then test on bilingual queries

No single embedding model is best. The 2026 shortlist is hosted APIs (Gemini embedding-001, Voyage, Cohere embed-v4, OpenAI text-embedding-3) and open weights. **Qwen3-Embedding-8B scores 70.58 on multilingual MTEB under Apache-2.0, with 32K context**, while BGE-M3 (MIT) produces dense, sparse and multi-vector output from one model ([PremAI](https://www.premai.io/blog/best-embedding-models-for-rag-2026-ranked-by-mteb-score-cost-and-self-hosting)). Watch the licenses. NV-Embed-v2 and Jina's weights are non-commercial, which rules them out of a paid self-hosted offering. Qwen3-Embedding serves directly from vLLM with `vllm serve Qwen/Qwen3-Embedding-8B --runner pooling` behind an OpenAI-compatible `/embeddings` endpoint, and the same family includes 0.6B, 4B and 8B rerankers ([vLLM docs](https://docs.vllm.ai/projects/ascend/en/latest/tutorials/Qwen3_embedding.html)). Queries should carry a one-sentence task instruction, which is worth 1–5% ([PremAI](https://www.premai.io/blog/best-embedding-models-for-rag-2026-ranked-by-mteb-score-cost-and-self-hosting)).

Treat MTEB as directional. It averages roughly eight task types and only retrieval predicts RAG quality ([techsy.io](https://techsy.io/en/blog/mteb-score-explained-rag)). Training on benchmark-adjacent data inflates scores ([Particula](https://particula.tech/blog/mteb-leaderboard-embedding-benchmark-contamination)), and secondary sources even disagree on who currently leads the English board. For Quebec, French itself is not the risk, because every shortlisted model handles a high-resource language. The real risks are **cross-lingual retrieval** (a French question over English documents) and **domain French**: Quebec legal and administrative vocabulary, anglicisms, abbreviations. No independent French retrieval benchmark for 2026 surfaced, so the only defensible method is to test candidates on 50–200 real bilingual client queries.

### pgvector is enough for nearly every mid-size client

Practitioners broadly agree that the choice of vector store matters less than chunking, embeddings and reranking. **pgvector is "production grade" up to roughly 5–10M vectors** as long as the HNSW index fits in RAM ([alexcloudstar](https://alexcloudstar.com/blog/vector-database-comparison-2026/); [noqta.tn](https://noqta.tn/en/blog/vector-database-comparison-2026-pgvector-pinecone-qdrant-weaviate)). A mid-size organization's document corpus, at a few hundred thousand to a few million chunks, sits comfortably inside that range. pgvector also gives you what a dedicated store cannot: joins to business data, row-level security and transactional consistency with the document ledger.

Its historical weakness was filtered search, and here a concrete example helps. With approximate HNSW search, the index explores a fixed number of candidates (`ef_search`, default 40) and *then* applies your `WHERE` clause. If your filter matches 10% of rows, a `LIMIT 10` query returns **only about 4 rows on average**, because most of the 40 candidates were filtered away ([pgEdge](https://docs.pgedge.com/pgvector/v0-8-0/iterative-index-scans/)). With permission filters, that failure is silent and serious. **pgvector 0.8.0 fixed most of it with iterative index scans**: set `hnsw.iterative_scan = relaxed_order` and the index keeps scanning until enough rows pass the filter, up to `hnsw.max_scan_tuples` ([PostgreSQL.org](https://www.postgresql.org/about/news/pgvector-080-released-2952/)). For very selective filters, such as one user's private folder, the maintainers advise skipping the ANN index entirely: an exact scan gives 100% recall. Independent research finds Postgres's planner often wrongly prefers the approximate index in exactly those cases ([arXiv 2602.11443](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11443v1)), so test your filtered queries with `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` rather than trusting the planner. Hybrid search in Postgres is pgvector plus `tsvector` full-text search, or a BM25 extension like ParadeDB's pg_search or VectorChord-bm25, fused with RRF in SQL ([Crunchy Data](https://www.crunchydata.com/blog/hybrid-vector-search)). One gap matters to Quebec clients: built-in `tsvector` with a French dictionary does stemming, but it is not true BM25. Benchmark both on bilingual queries before committing.

Move off pgvector at hundreds of millions of vectors, with heavy multi-tenancy, when you need native multi-vector (ColBERT-style) search, or when you need sub-20ms p99 latency ([ClickHouse](https://clickhouse.com/resources/engineering/scale-vector-search-postgres)). Qdrant is the self-hosted step up, at the cost of running "two databases and sync logic" ([encore.dev](https://encore.dev/articles/pgvector-vs-qdrant)). turbopuffer and Pinecone are managed options; Linear reportedly cut costs 70% moving from Elasticsearch plus pgvector to turbopuffer ([Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/turbopuffer)). No independent, reproducible 2026 benchmark across stores exists, so treat every vendor comparison as marketing.

### Rerankers have converged; pick by hosting constraint

Hosted rerankers (Cohere Rerank v3.5/v4, Voyage rerank-2.5/3) and open ones (Qwen3-Reranker, bge-reranker-v2-m3, Jina) are now within a few percent of each other on vendor-reported numbers. Voyage claims rerank-3 beats Qwen3-Reranker-8B by **3.31% across 93 datasets** ([OpenRouter/Voyage](https://openrouter.ai/voyageai/rerank-3)). Even taken at face value, a self-hosted reranker gives up very little. No independent head-to-head exists, and no 2026 latency or cost data surfaced. For a private stack, Qwen3-Reranker on vLLM is the natural choice because it shares a serving layer with your embeddings and generator.

### Query transformation is situational, and agents are absorbing it

The one query transformation that has become a default is cheap. An LLM rewrites a conversational follow-up ("what about for contractors?") into a standalone query and extracts metadata filters (year, department, document type). HyDE embeds a hypothetical answer instead of the question. It helped the TREC 2025 winner, but its individual contribution wasn't isolated ([TREC UTokyo](https://trec.nist.gov/pubs/trec34/papers/UTokyo.rag.pdf)), and it adds an LLM call to every query. Decomposition helps multi-hop questions but "remains limited by the quality of query decomposition" ([TREC uogTr](https://trec.nist.gov/pubs/trec34/papers/uogTr.rag.pdf)). Production data adds a warning. On the Danish National Encyclopedia's live system, benchmarks suggested about 90% of queries needed LLM augmentation, but **only about 28% of real traffic benefited** ([arXiv 2605.27220](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.27220)). Route to these techniques only when a measured query segment needs them. No controlled 2025–2026 study compares HyDE, multi-query, decomposition and step-back on a common benchmark.

## GraphRAG, agents and long context are routing decisions, not upgrades

The three most-hyped alternatives to the baseline each win in a specific situation and lose outside it. The consultant's skill is recognizing which situation the client is actually in.

**GraphRAG** has an LLM extract entities and relationships into a graph with community summaries, so the system can answer corpus-wide "sensemaking" questions like "what themes recur across 5,000 incident reports?" It is expensive. In an independent benchmark, building the full Microsoft GraphRAG index consumed about **79.9M tokens and 11,181 seconds, versus about 10.1M tokens for RAPTOR** ([arXiv 2506.02404](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.02404)). Microsoft's LazyGraphRAG cuts indexing to vector-RAG cost and claims to match global search at over 700 times lower query cost, but those are Microsoft's own numbers ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/lazygraphrag/)). Independent work finds graph methods often underperform plain RAG on simple fact lookup and calls lightweight frameworks such as LightRAG "a far more cost-effective solution" ([arXiv 2506.05690](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.05690)). The strongest production case remains LinkedIn's graph built from support tickets: **77.6% higher MRR and a 28.6% cut in median resolution time** ([arXiv 2404.17723](https://arxiv.com/abs/2404.17723)). That worked because the tickets had real structure to exploit. Recommend graphs only when users genuinely ask corpus-wide or entity-chain questions and a hybrid-plus-rerank baseline has measurably failed.

**Agentic RAG** turns retrieval into a tool that an LLM calls repeatedly. The agent searches, reads, reformulates, filters and searches again, instead of doing one retrieve-then-generate pass. Claude Code famously dropped its vector index for grep and file reads because "agentic search generally works better" and needs no index to keep in sync ([AI Engineer](https://www.ai.engineer/talks/UM6sFg_jdlE-rag-is-dead-right-kuba-rogut)). Hamel Husain points out that the "RAG is dead" claim targets naive vector retrieval for coding agents, not retrieval in general ([hamel.dev](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/is-rag-dead.html)), and RAG co-author Douwe Kiela says RAG "is still happening, but it's not called that anymore," now going by "context engineering" ([The New Stack](https://thenewstack.io/tns-daily/january-27-2026/)). The tradeoff is concrete. An agent fixes its own bad queries and follows references across documents, but it costs 5–20 tool calls and many seconds per question. That is why teams gate it behind cheap routing ([Atolio](https://www.atolio.com/blog/assembling-and-evolving-an-enterprise-rag-platform)). Menlo found **only 16% of enterprise deployments qualify as "true agents"** ([Menlo](https://menlovc.com/perspective/2025-the-state-of-generative-ai-in-the-enterprise/)), and Gartner predicts over 40% of agentic projects will be cancelled by 2027 over cost and unclear value ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-predicts-over-40-percent-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-canceled-by-end-of-2027)). The practical design is to expose hybrid search (plus SQL or metadata filters) as a tool for hard questions, and keep a fast single-shot path for high-volume FAQ traffic. No rigorous benchmark compares the two approaches on non-code enterprise corpora with cost and latency included. One caveat applies to your setup: a small model like Qwen3-8B is a weaker tool-calling agent than frontier models, so test agentic loops on the client's questions before promising them. That is my inference, not a measured result.

**Long context** has not replaced retrieval. Chroma's "Context Rot" study ran 194,480 calls across 18 models and found **performance degraded with input length in every experiment**. On LongMemEval, focused ~300-token prompts beat full ~113K-token prompts for every model family ([Chroma](https://trychroma.com/research/context-rot)). The ICML 2025 LaRA study concludes there is "No Silver Bullet": the winner depends on model, length, task and retrieval quality ([arXiv 2502.09977](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.09977v1)). Google DeepMind's "Self-Route" lets the model decide whether retrieved chunks suffice and falls back to full context otherwise, keeping most of the quality at much lower cost ([arXiv 2407.16833](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.16833v1)). The usable rule comes from Anthropic: **if the knowledge base is under about 200K tokens (roughly 500 pages), skip RAG** and put it all in a cached prompt ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/contextual-retrieval)). For a private deployment the caching economics differ. vLLM's prefix caching helps, but a 16 GB card cannot hold a 200K-token context for an 8B model, so the small-corpus shortcut mainly applies to managed or larger self-hosted setups. That last point is my inference about your hardware, not a sourced figure.

## Evaluation is most of the work, and most teams skip it

Evaluation is the stage that separates consultants who deliver from consultants who demo. Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar report that **60–80% of development time** in their projects went to error analysis and evaluation ([hamel.dev](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/why-is-error-analysis-so-important-in-llm-evals-and-how-is-it-performed.html)), and Uber built an LLM judge precisely because waiting weeks for SME review made iteration impossible ([Uber](https://www.uber.com/blog/enhanced-agentic-rag/)). One practitioner put the stakes bluntly: "Teams without an eval set have a chatbot with a search bar" ([Looming Tech](https://www.looming.tech/post/rag-in-production-lessons-enterprise-deployments)).

### Measure retrieval as search first, then generation with validated judges

The consensus method splits the problem in two. Retrieval is scored like a search engine: given labelled question-to-document pairs, measure **recall@k** (did the right document appear in the top k?) and MRR (how high did it rank?). Only after retrieval is healthy do you score generation, using the "RAG triad": context relevance (is the retrieved context about the question?), faithfulness (does the answer stick to the context?) and answer relevance (does it answer the question?) ([hamel.dev](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)). Pairing context recall with faithfulness localizes every wrong answer. If the needed fact never reached the model, it is a retrieval failure. If it reached the model and the model drifted, it is a generation failure ([Ragas paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.15217)). A third bucket catches the failures clients don't expect. **An answer can score perfectly on faithfulness and still be wrong** when the index serves a repealed policy alongside the current one ([Atlan](https://atlan.com/know/how-to-evaluate-rag-systems-explained/)). That is a content-governance problem, not a model problem, and it should be named as such in reports.

Off-the-shelf generation metrics deserve skepticism. An independent July 2026 study compared Ragas, DeepEval, RAGChecker and Opik against human raters on **96 French questions over 479 telecom documents**, which is unusually relevant for a Quebec practice. Reference-free faithfulness and hallucination metrics correlated weakly with human judgment, RAGChecker's recall-style metrics did best, and Opik's moderation metric showed no correlation. The author cautions that one system and wide confidence intervals mean it should not be used to rank tools ([arXiv 2607.07302](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.07302)). TREC's large-scale work shows the same shape: LLM-automated "nugget" scoring agrees strongly with human assessors **at the system level**, but weakly per topic ([arXiv 2504.15068](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.15068v1)). Translated for clients, LLM judges are good for "is config B better than config A across 200 questions?" and unreliable for "was this particular answer correct?"

### Build eval sets from real questions; synthetic data flatters

Synthetic test questions are systematically easier than real ones. A 2026 study of a university system found real queries averaged **6.8 words versus 15.7 for synthetic ones**, and configurations that looked strong on synthetic data dropped noticeably on real traffic ([arXiv 2609.14579](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.14579)). Another found synthetic benchmarks gave uniformly high context precision while human-written ones actually separated retrieval settings ([arXiv 2508.11758](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.11758)). The workable pattern has three steps. Before launch, seed 50–100 questions mixing SME-written and structured synthetic ones; generate synthetic questions from defined dimensions such as persona × topic × difficulty, never by prompting for generic "test questions" ([hamel.dev](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)). Report those scores as an upper bound. After launch, replace them with sampled real queries and grow the set from every thumbs-down and escalation.

LLM judges should give binary pass/fail verdicts rather than 1–5 scales, with one judge per failure mode. Validate each judge against held-out human labels by measuring its true-positive and true-negative rates, then use those rates to correct the failure rate you report ([hamel.dev](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)). Budget roughly 100–200 labelled examples per judge. Don't write the rubric first: reading outputs changes what reviewers think "good" means. Shankar's research calls this "criteria drift" ([arXiv 2404.12272](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.12272)). The defensible sentence in a client report sounds like "judge validated on 80 held-out labels, TPR 0.91, TNR 0.86, corrected failure rate 7% ± 3%." A raw judge score with no validation is the anti-pattern.

### Tooling consolidated in 2026; the process matters more than the vendor

The market split into metric libraries that run in code or CI (Ragas, DeepEval, TruLens, promptfoo) and tracing-plus-eval platforms (Langfuse, Arize Phoenix, LangSmith, Braintrust, MLflow 3). 2026 brought consolidation: **ClickHouse acquired Langfuse in January, OpenAI announced its acquisition of Promptfoo in March, and Cisco closed its Galileo acquisition in May** ([Langfuse](https://langfuse.com/blog/joining-clickhouse); [Security Boulevard](https://securityboulevard.com/2026/03/openai-acquires-security-startup-promptfoo-to-fortify-ai-agents); [AgentMarketCap](https://agentmarketcap.ai/blog/2026/04/13/cisco-acquires-galileo-ai-agent-observability-enterprise-monitoring-consolidation-2026)). For private deployments the choice is narrow. **Langfuse is MIT-licensed and fully self-hostable** (Postgres, ClickHouse, Redis, S3) ([Langfuse](https://langfuse.com/compare)). Phoenix is lighter but source-available under the Elastic License, not OSI open source. LangSmith and Braintrust self-host only on enterprise plans. Husain's view is that the major platforms are broadly similar and support quality often decides ([hamel.dev](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)). Production monitoring has converged into one loop: OpenTelemetry traces of every stage, asynchronous judges on sampled live traffic, a human review queue, the golden set, and a small CI regression suite dominated by deterministic assertions. Ragas's own docs still list mining implicit user signals as "on the roadmap" ([Ragas](https://docs.ragas.io/en/latest/getstarted/monitoring.html)), so treat thumbs and click data as triage signals, not quality metrics.

## Permissions, not models, are the main security risk

The most important security finding of the last two years is that enterprise RAG mostly leaks through **oversharing in the source systems**, not through the AI layer. Microsoft 365 Copilot respects permissions faithfully, which means years of "Everyone except external users" links and inherited SharePoint permissions become answerable by plain-language question. In the words of one analysis, "Copilot didn't overshare your data. Your permissions did" ([Petri](https://petri.com/copilot-didnt-overshare-your-data-your-permissions-did/)). A Gartner survey found oversharing concerns led **40% of IT leaders to delay Copilot rollouts by three months or more** ([Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/3542000/microsoft-365-copilot-rollouts-slowed-by-data-security-roi-concerns.html)). Say this explicitly in every pitch: a self-hosted RAG with perfect ACL mirroring leaks just as Copilot does if the underlying SharePoint is overshared. "Private" removes the third-party processor; it does not fix permissions.

### The industry converged on one enforcement pattern

Every serious platform does roughly the same thing. Copy source ACLs (users, groups, allow and deny) into the index at ingestion. Filter on them *inside* the search query. Optionally re-verify against the source per result. Fail closed. Authenticate the user upstream. Post-filtering, where you retrieve first and then drop unauthorized chunks, is considered unsafe, because the model may already have seen restricted text and because authorized results just past the top k are lost ([tianpan.co](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026/04/17/vector-store-access-control-rag-rls)). AWS's Bedrock managed knowledge base pre-filters on crawled ACLs and then makes a real-time check against SharePoint, Drive or Confluence per document. AWS also states plainly that this is **"ACL-aware filtering, not a security boundary,"** because Bedrock does not authenticate end users. Its identity model keys on email, so a mismatched address "fails silently" ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/kb-managed-acl.html)). Azure AI Search added native Entra-based document ACLs, including SharePoint ACL ingestion, but as of the latest docs SharePoint groups are not supported and preview API versions remain. Permission changes appear only after the next indexer run ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-za/azure/search/search-indexer-sharepoint-access-control-lists)).

For your stack, the Postgres version is natural. Store `acl_principals text[]` on each chunk, set the caller's principal set as a session variable per request, and let a row-level security policy filter every similarity search "in the database kernel, not in application code." Two traps are documented. A connection pool that doesn't reset the session variable leaks one user's context into the next request, and RLS can work against the HNSW index, which you mitigate with partitioning or partial indexes ([tianpan.co](https://tianpan.co/blog/2026-04-17-vector-store-access-control-rag-rls)). Combine this with pgvector 0.8's iterative scans, or a narrow-access user silently gets three results instead of ten. The genuinely hard part is not the filter. It is **identity and group resolution**: expanding nested Entra or AD groups, mapping identities across systems, and the staleness window between permission syncs. One documented answer is a permission service such as OpenFGA, so that revocation takes effect on the next query without re-indexing ([wavect.io](https://wavect.io/blog/rag-permissions-sharepoint-confluence-drive/)).

### Retrieved text is an attack surface

OWASP's 2025 Top 10 for LLM applications added **LLM08: Vector and Embedding Weaknesses** specifically for RAG. It covers unauthorized access, cross-tenant leaks, embedding inversion and poisoning, and it makes the point that an unprotected vector store is functionally an unprotected database ([IronCore Labs](https://ironcorelabs.com/blog/2025/owasp-llm-top10-2025-update/)). The canonical real-world case is **EchoLeak (CVE-2025-32711, CVSS 9.3)**. A crafted email, retrieved into Copilot's context, carried hidden instructions, and data left through Markdown image and link rendering via an allowed Teams proxy. It needed zero user interaction, and it bypassed Microsoft's injection classifier, link redaction and CSP ([arXiv 2509.10540](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.10540v1)). Poisoning is cheap: **five planted texts per target question achieved about 90% attack success** in a corpus of millions ([PoisonedRAG](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.12784v1)). Embeddings are not anonymized either. Vec2Text recovered 92% of 32-token inputs exactly from their vectors ([Vec2Text mitigation study](https://astro.paperswithcode.com/paper/understanding-and-mitigating-the-threat-of)). Under Quebec privacy law, the safest assumption is that embeddings of personal information are personal information, although no regulator has said so. The self-hosted UI layer has also shipped RCE-class bugs: **Open WebUI CVE-2025-64496** (fixed in 0.6.35) and **RAGFlow CVE-2025-68700** (fixed in 0.23.0) ([NVD](https://nvd.nist.gov/vuln/detail/CVE-2025-64496); [OSV](https://osv.dev/vulnerability/CVE-2025-68700)).

No single defense is reliable, so practice is layered. Enforce ACLs before retrieval. Treat all retrieved text as untrusted data. Kill the exfiltration channel by never rendering model-generated external URLs or images and by blocking egress from the inference host. Restrict who can write to indexed sources, since poisoning requires write access. Encrypt and access-control the vector store like the source data. Log retrievals per user. Patch the open-source UI aggressively. These are synthesized from the incidents above rather than drawn from a single benchmark; no independent 2026 comparison of prompt-injection defenses exists.

### Canadian residency is the strongest argument for self-hosting

Managed options are viable in Canada, but residency depends on deployment type, and the newest models often aren't available in-region. Azure OpenAI *Global* and *Data Zone* deployments can process inference outside Canada ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/AZURE/ai-services/openai/how-to/deployment-types)). Only *Regional* deployments in Canada East (Québec City) or Canada Central keep it in-region, and in July 2026 a Microsoft Q&A thread reported **no GPT-5.1 Regional deployment in either Canadian region** ([MS Q&A](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/answers/questions/5935539/need-help-understanding-the-options-and-road-map-f)). AWS reaches Claude from ca-central-1 via cross-region inference to US or Global profiles. Data at rest stays in Canada; inference processing does not ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/accelerate-generative-ai-innovation-in-canada-with-amazon-bedrock-cross-region-inference)). Even AWS's "Canada" geography for one service includes three US regions ([AWS AgentCore](https://docs.amazonaws.cn/bedrock-agentcore/latest/devguide/cross-region-inference.html)).

That tension feeds directly into **Quebec Law 25 s.17**, which requires a privacy impact assessment before personal information is communicated outside Quebec, with penalties up to CAD 10M or 2% of worldwide turnover ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2023/9/quebecs-law-25-many-provisions-take-effect-today)). One practitioner reading applies it "in principle" to any cloud whose servers are outside Quebec, *including Canadian ones* ([Hilo](https://hilotech.ca/en/wiki/law-25/cross-border-transfers)). That is an interpretation, not a position of the Commission d'accès à l'information, the Quebec privacy regulator. A GPU server physically in Quebec, on premises or in a Quebec colocation facility, sidesteps the cross-border analysis for the inference path. The federal Guide on the use of generative AI points the same way for public-sector clients: no sensitive information in tools "not managed by the GC," approval from the chief security officer for protected information, and an explicit endorsement of grounding outputs in institution-controlled data ([Canada.ca](https://www.canada.ca/en/government/system/digital-government/digital-government-innovations/responsible-use-ai/guide-use-generative-ai.html)). The common middle ground is hybrid: private parsing, embeddings, index and ACL enforcement, plus a managed Regional LLM endpoint in Canada East, which gives a residency story that is easy to explain. Legal advice on s.17 and on the separate PIA requirement for new information systems belongs with the client's privacy counsel. These notes did not verify the s.3.3 text.

## A private reference stack that maps onto what you already run

Self-hosted options sort into tiers. **Onyx** (formerly Danswer; MIT core, enterprise license for some features) is the open-source option closest to Glean: it syncs content *and permissions* from Confluence, Jira, Drive, Slack, SharePoint and others into a hybrid index ([wz-it.com](https://wz-it.com/en/blog/onyx-self-hosted-installation/)). The connector permission claims come from an agency write-up and should be verified against Onyx's docs. **Open WebUI** is a polished chat front end with SSO, RBAC and SCIM in its free edition, but its RAG covers manually uploaded files only ([Open WebUI docs](https://docs.openwebui.com/alternatives/onyx)). Dify and RAGFlow are capable builder platforms that need hardening. LlamaIndex and Haystack are frameworks for a custom build where you own the ACL logic. AnythingLLM, PrivateGPT and Kotaemon are best treated as single-team or demo grade. That tiering is my synthesis, since no independent production-readiness comparison exists.

For a consultant with your skills, the strongest offering is a custom build on Postgres. Postgres is the piece you can operate, explain and defend, and its RLS turns permission enforcement into a database guarantee rather than an application promise. Use Onyx when the client's main need is "search across our SaaS tools with permissions" and the budget doesn't support custom connectors. The recommended reference stack:

| Layer | Recommendation | Why / notes |
|---|---|---|
| Identity | Client's Entra ID, Okta or Keycloak via OIDC with group claims | Single identity source; resolve nested groups at sync time |
| Connectors + ACL capture | Microsoft Graph API, Drive API, Confluence REST, custom; or Onyx connectors | Capture allow/deny principals with every document |
| Parsing service | Docling via docling-serve (Python container); VLM OCR (PaddleOCR-VL, MinerU2.5 or olmOCR) on vLLM for scans and tables | Behind a swappable interface; pick by testing client documents |
| Queue + storage | BullMQ or Postgres-backed queue; S3-compatible object store (MinIO on-prem) for raw files and parsed JSON | Parsing is slow and bursty; keep intermediates to re-chunk cheaply |
| Chunking + enrichment (TypeScript) | Heading-aware 200–512 tokens, tables whole, heading path in each chunk, deterministic headers; optional LLM contextualization with local model | Best-evidenced quality lever after parsing |
| Ledger | `documents(id, source_uri, hash, version, status, acl_principals)` → `chunks` FK with cascade delete; chunk-level hashes | Incremental sync, deletes, blue/green re-embeds |
| Embeddings | Qwen3-Embedding (0.6B for a lab, 4B/8B for production) on vLLM `--runner pooling`; BGE-M3 if sparse vectors are wanted | Apache/MIT licenses; strong multilingual; test on FR/EN queries |
| Store + hybrid | Postgres + pgvector ≥0.8 (HNSW, `iterative_scan = relaxed_order`) + French/English FTS or pg_search; RRF in SQL; RLS on `acl_principals` | One database for vectors, BM25, ACLs, joins and transactions |
| Reranker | Qwen3-Reranker or bge-reranker-v2-m3 on the same serving layer | Retrieve ~100, rerank to ~10 |
| Generator | vLLM serving an open-weight Qwen, Mistral or Llama model sized to the client's GPU and quality bar | Qwen3-8B is fine for a lab; size up for production synthesis |
| Orchestration | TypeScript service exposing single-shot RAG and a `search` tool for an agentic path, gated by a router | Cheap path for FAQs; agents only where measured |
| Front end | Custom app, or Open WebUI pinned to patched versions | Strip external image and link rendering |
| Observability + eval | Langfuse self-hosted (OTel traces); Ragas or DeepEval to start, replaced by validated binary judges; CI regression suite | Process over tools |
| Controls | Egress-blocked inference host, per-user retrieval audit log, ingestion source allowlist, patch cadence, red-team pass | Covers OWASP LLM01/02/08 |

Your home rig is the right place to build and demo this stack, and the wrong place for client data. A single RTX 5070 Ti (16 GB) reached over Tailscale is a credible lab for proving the pipeline, running Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B and a small reranker beside a quantized 8B generator, and benchmarking parsers on sample documents. A client deployment belongs on the client's own hardware or in a Quebec data centre. Showing up with that distinction already drawn is itself a trust signal in a residency-driven sale. The hardware sizing in this paragraph is my engineering judgment, not a sourced benchmark.

## Production teams win on process; engagements are sold in phases

The failure evidence points away from technology. MIT NANDA's widely quoted finding, that about **95% of GenAI pilots show no measurable P&L impact**, is attributed to a "learning gap" and poor workflow integration, not model quality. It rests on a small qualitative base and is often over-quoted ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/08/18/mit-report-95-percent-generative-ai-pilots-at-companies-failing-cfo/)). Klarna's CEO admitted that cost was "a too-predominant evaluation factor" and quality suffered, and the company resumed hiring human agents ([Maginative](https://www.maginative.com/article/klarna-dials-back-its-ai-customer-service-strategy-now-its-hiring-humans-again/)). The well-evidenced failure causes are oversharing, bad or stale source documents, no evaluation set, the wrong success metric and agentic cost overruns. RAG-specific "70% of deployments fail" statistics circulate widely but trace to unverifiable blog claims, so don't repeat them.

### Build versus buy: don't compete with Copilot on "search all our docs"

Enterprises shifted from **47% building in 2024 to 24% in 2025** because bought tools reach production faster ([Menlo](https://menlovc.com/perspective/2025-the-state-of-generative-ai-in-the-enterprise/)). Price signals from third parties show what you are competing with: Microsoft 365 Copilot at about $30 per user per month, Gemini Enterprise at about $21–60+, ChatGPT Enterprise reportedly $45–75 with seat minimums ([Redress Compliance](https://redresscompliance.com/google-gemini-vs-copilot-vs-openai-enterprise-cost.html)), and Onyx's enterprise tier around $16–20 ([Kestra](https://kestra.io/resources/ai/glean-alternatives)). Verify these before quoting them. Custom work still wins in five places: corpora with heavy structure and exact identifiers (Harvey's legal retrieval mixes citations, docket numbers and implicit jurisdiction filters, per [AI Engineer](https://www.ai.engineer/talks/W1MiZChnkfA-scaling-enterprise-grade-rag-systems-lessons)), workflow-embedded uses with a measurable outcome, strict residency or self-hosting requirements, integration with bought platforms, and readiness work. Readiness means the permissions audit, content cleanup and an evaluation of the client's licensed Copilot on their own questions. Clients need it whether they build or buy, so it is the easiest first sale.

### Engagements run discovery, pilot, hardening, retainer

Jason Liu's consulting guide describes the typical independent shape. A **six-week project** runs two weeks of discovery, three weeks of development and one week of handoff, with deployment explicitly excluded. That is followed by a maintenance retainer of about $3–5K/month light, $7–12K active or $15–25K strategic. Implementation projects run $25–100K, and new consultants should start at $7–10K minimums, raising $1–2K after each yes ([567 Labs](https://567-labs.github.io/consulting/guide/16-engagement-models/); [567 Labs pricing](https://567-labs.github.io/consulting/guide/07-hourly-to-value/)). Canadian rate signals, all from firms selling these services, put senior boutique AI consulting at **CAD 200–350/hour** and small knowledge-base chatbots at CAD 4–18K ([ChatGPT.ca](https://chatgpt.ca/blog/ai-consulting-rate-card-canada-2026)). Broader GenAI builds run CAD 60–160K+ over 4–8 months ([Apptunix](https://www.apptunix.com/blog/ai-software-development-cost-canada/)).

Combining those sources, a plausible ladder for a mid-size Quebec client is a paid discovery and data audit at CAD 10–25K (1–2 weeks), then an eval-set-plus-baseline phase and pilot at CAD 30–80K (4–8 weeks), then production hardening (SSO and ACL sync, monitoring, re-indexing, red-teaming), then a CAD 3–10K/month retainer. The whole arc runs three to six months. That ladder is my synthesis rather than a published benchmark, and team size, typically one or two people from your side plus client SMEs and an IT/security owner, is likewise inferred. Liu's structural safeguards are worth copying from day one: one shared channel, deliverables in the client's repository, one designated client contact, and an explicit scope list.

### What to say and ask in discovery

The highest-leverage discovery conversation targets the things clients consistently underestimate: SME time for evaluation, document extraction, permissions hygiene and ongoing operations ([Looming Tech](https://www.looming.tech/post/rag-in-production-lessons-enterprise-deployments)). The list below is synthesized from the failure modes above rather than taken from a published checklist.

| Say or ask | What it surfaces |
|---|---|
| "What decision or task gets faster or better, and what number proves it?" | Success metric; avoids the Klarna trap of optimizing cost alone |
| "Send me 30–50 real questions from last month, with the right answer and where it lives." | Seeds the eval set; tests whether the answers exist in documents at all |
| "Where do the sources live, in what formats, and how are superseded versions marked?" | Parsing difficulty (scans, tables) and the stale-policy failure class |
| "Do answers have to respect per-user permissions? When did anyone last audit 'Everyone' shares?" | ACL scope; sells the oversharing audit |
| "Which Copilot, Google, ChatGPT or Glean licences do you already own, and have you tested them on these questions?" | Build-vs-buy honesty; often the cheapest first deliverable |
| "Does any personal information leave Quebec today? Is a hosted model in Canada East acceptable?" | Law 25 s.17 exposure; private vs hybrid architecture |
| "Which SMEs can give us two to four hours a week for evaluation?" | The single most underestimated cost |
| "What happens when the system is wrong, and who is accountable?" | Escalation path, risk appetite, need for citations and abstention |
| "Who owns re-indexing, monitoring and regression tests after handoff?" | Retainer scope and long-term viability |
| Say: "Private AI removes the third-party processor, not prompt injection or oversharing." | Sets honest expectations and differentiates you from vendors |

## Conclusion

The center of gravity in RAG has moved from *retrieval technique* to *systems discipline*. In 2023 you could differentiate by knowing about hybrid search or reranking. In 2026 those are table stakes, documented in every vendor guide and absorbed into every platform. What remains scarce is the ability to make a specific organization's messy, permissioned, bilingual documents answerable, and to prove it with numbers a skeptical SME will accept. For a solo consultant that changes what to practise. Building a pipeline is the easy part. Running an error analysis on a hundred traces, validating a judge, and explaining to a director why their SharePoint permissions are the real risk are the skills that are rare.

The Quebec private-AI angle is stronger than generic RAG consulting precisely because it sits where the market's defaults break down. Frontier models lag in Canadian regions, and Law 25 turns "where does inference happen?" into a compliance question rather than a preference. A Postgres-centred, vLLM-served stack is something you can stand behind line by line. The open problems, namely degraded scans, prompt injection, poisoning, synthetic-eval optimism and agentic cost, are also where an honest consultant earns trust by naming the limits up front instead of discovering them in production.
